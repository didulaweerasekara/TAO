Listening is a leadership skill

We train people to speak, present and persuade. We rarely train them to listen, yet understanding the situation best is a decisive advantage.

We train people to speak: to pitch, present and persuade. We rarely train them to listen. Yet in most organisations the decisive advantage belongs to whoever understands the situation best, and understanding starts with attention.

Three levels of listening

At the first level we listen for a gap in which to speak. At the second we listen to understand: following the content, the logic and the facts. At the third we listen for what is not said: the hesitation, the emphasis, the concern behind the question. Leadership happens mostly at the third level.

Leaders are rarely short of answers. What they often lack is the patience to hear the real question.

Habits that build the skill

  • Put the device away. Attention is visible, and so is its absence.
  • Summarise before you respond. "What I'm hearing is…" slows the conversation down and prevents costly assumptions.
  • Ask open questions. "Tell me more about that" invites depth; "Did you try X?" invites defence.
  • Tolerate silence. The most important thing is often said after a pause.

Why it matters for trust

People do not need to agree with a decision in order to support it. They need to feel that their view was heard and weighed. Listening is the fastest way to build that trust, and the step most often skipped when the calendar is full.

Practise it

  • In your next one-to-one, aim to speak for less than a third of the time.
  • Before giving your view, summarise the other person's in a sentence and check it.
  • Notice what you learn that you would otherwise have missed.
