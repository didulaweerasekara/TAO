Working across cultures: five practical habits

Most misunderstandings in international teams are not about language. They are about assumptions.

Most misunderstandings in international teams are not about language. They are about assumptions — how direct to be, how much to explain, what silence means, who is expected to decide. Nobody is wrong; everybody is working from a different default.

1. Assume difference, not deficiency

When a colleague's behaviour puzzles you, ask what would make it reasonable from where they stand before you draw conclusions. That single question prevents a great deal of avoidable conflict.

2. Make the implicit explicit

In some cultures a hint is a clear instruction; in others it goes unnoticed. Say plainly what you need, by when and why, and invite the same clarity back. It can feel over-careful at first. It soon feels like relief.

3. Slow down the first conversation

Some business cultures put the relationship first; others put the task first. Spending real time getting to know people at the start of a collaboration is often the fastest route to speed later.

Clarity is a courtesy — especially when you do not share a first language.

4. Share the inconvenience

If every meeting suits one time zone, one language and one working style, the team is not really international. Rotate meeting times, share the burden of working outside normal hours and let different voices set the pace.

5. Agree how you will disagree

Some people raise objections openly; others do so privately or through questions. Agree early how disagreement will be raised and how it will be received, so that no style is penalised.

Practise it

  • At the end of your next cross-team meeting, restate the decisions, owners and dates in writing.
  • Ask one colleague from a different background how they prefer to receive feedback.
  • Check whether your meeting times always suit the same people.
