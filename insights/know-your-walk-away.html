Know your walk‑away before you walk in

Most negotiations are won or lost before anyone speaks. Preparation starts with knowing what you will do if there is no deal.

Most negotiations are decided before anyone speaks. The side that has prepared better — that knows what it needs, what the other side needs and what it will do if there is no agreement — usually does better. Not because it is tougher, but because it is clearer.

Start with your walk-away

Negotiation practitioners call it your BATNA: your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. The term comes from Roger Fisher and William Ury's Getting to Yes, and the idea is simple. Before you negotiate, decide what you will actually do if this deal does not happen.

A clear alternative changes how you behave at the table. Without one, every offer feels like one you cannot refuse. With one, you can listen calmly, test proposals against something real and walk away when you should.

Then think about theirs

The other side has an alternative too. The stronger it is, the less they need you; the weaker it is, the more room you have. Time spent understanding their position — their pressures, deadlines and constraints — is rarely wasted.

You cannot negotiate well for something you have not defined.

Separate positions from interests

A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Two parties arguing about price may both care, underneath, about certainty, timing or reputation. Agreements that last are usually built on interests, because interests leave room for options that positions do not.

Improve your alternative before you improve your pitch

If your walk-away is weak, the most useful preparation may not be a better argument but a better alternative: another supplier, another partner, another route to the same goal. Negotiating power often comes from options you create before the conversation begins.

Practise it

  • Before your next negotiation, write down your BATNA in one sentence.
  • Write down your best estimate of theirs.
  • List three interests behind your position — and three you suspect lie behind theirs.
